# Bullet Tweets — Drafting Instructions

A consolidated record of every explicit instruction and correction given for drafting bullet tweets. These are the user's rules, not general advice. They override default tendencies.

---

## What a Bullet Tweet Is

A long-form X/Twitter post used to promote a Swarajya article. Built around three things: a sharp opening, structured bullet sections, and a handoff to the article. The post is **not** a summary — it is an argument built from the article's evidence designed to make the reader click.

---

## Openings

### Must do

- **Hook the reader.** The opening has to grab attention.
- **Feel informal.** Conversational register, not press-release. Not LinkedIn.
- **Be specific to the article.** Generic openers fail.
- **Use a peg the article itself supplies** when one is explicitly requested — e.g. "begin with people criticising Modi for legacy node," "use China's nuclear posture as the opening peg," "make it about Vijay's allies and his own history."

### Must avoid

- **"It wasn't just X. It was Y." constructions.** Sounds canned and self-important.
- **Borrowed framings from the article that do no real work.** ("Pure cinema. The business end of governance won't be.") Decorative without earning their place.
- **Openers with no story arc.** Two stacked sections without a build is not an opening.
- **Weak, abstract openers** that don't establish stakes or specificity in the first sentence.

---

## Story Arc

- **Every post needs a story arc.** Sections that stack without building are a failure.
- **The arc moves**: setup → development → consequence → handoff. Or: problem → cause → solution. Or: news peg → background → why it's different → significance.
- If a post has parallel sections (Threat 1, Threat 2) with no momentum between them, it has no arc.

---

## Bullet Sections

### Structure

- **2–4 labelled sections**, each with 3–5 bullets.
- Section headers can be questions, declarative phrases, or thematic tags — "Why this matters strategically:", "Sources of dysfunction:", "How the wall got built:", "What works elsewhere:".

### Bullet length

- **Bullets must be short.** When drafted as full prose sentences, the result feels "unbulleted" — defeats the format.
- Compress to fact-per-line. Use parentheticals for the killer detail.
- Don't pad with qualifier-first phrasing. Lead with the punchy fact.

### Bullet content

- **Every bullet carries data, not opinion.** Numbers, comparisons, named entities, dates.
- **Comparative scale is the strongest bullet form** ("India spends 0.8% of GDP on R&D. South Korea spent over 4%. China invests $335 billion annually. India invests $7 billion.").
- If a bullet could be deleted without losing a fact, delete it.

---

## Length Control

- **The user dictates length.** When asked for shorter, cut hard — not by a few words.
- "Slightly shorter" = trim repetition and tighten prose, keep all sections.
- "Shorter version" = collapse to 8–10 bullets, single section if needed.
- "60% of length" / "50% of last one's length" = literal proportional cut. Take the percentage seriously.
- "Small bullet tweet" / "short bullet tweet" = compact, fewer sections, tighter bullets.

---

## Voice and Register

### Keep

- **Informal but informed.** "Not surprised by yet another NEET paper leak" + "the predictable output of an exam system that is brittle by design" in the same paragraph.
- **Em dashes for compression.**
- **Aphoristic closers** in rule-of-three form — "Investors tolerate corruption. They tolerate bureaucracy. They do not tolerate uncertainty." / "The measurement exists. The capital exists. The institution that does the work does not."
- **Sentence fragments for emphasis** when they fit the rhythm.

### Avoid

- "Make no mistake" / "Let's be clear" / "At the end of the day" — filler.
- "Game-changer" / "watershed moment" / "pure cinema" — decorative.
- Press-release tone.
- LinkedIn thought-leadership tone.
- Phrases like "is undergoing its biggest transformation since…" when the brief is to lead with a different angle.

---

## Handoff Line

- Always names the publication: `@swarajyamag`.
- Names the author when the article warrants it: "by Prakhar Gupta," "by Karan Kamble," "by Anmol N Jain."
- Frames the article's distinctive value — "the sharpest diagnosis you'll read," "the clearest explanation," "the best explainer you'll find."
- Ends with the link.
- **Never** end with generic "worth a read" or "check it out."

## Specific Corrections Encountered

These are the actual rewrites the user asked for. They are the strongest signals about what to avoid.

| Draft contained | User instruction | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| "Vijay's oath as Tamil Nadu CM was pure cinema…" | "why do we need this opening? there is no story arc in the post" | Borrowed framings without arc fail. |
| Rewritten opener: "Tamil Nadu's industrial engine — 9% of national manufacturing…" | "the opening is weak and doesn't hook, doesn't feel informal enough" | Lead with the most pointed, specific, informal observation available. |
| "The Agni MIRV test on 8 May wasn't just another missile launch — it was India crossing a strategic threshold…" | "avoid language like 'wasn't just another. it was x'" | Kill the construction permanently. |
| Reframed with India-first angle | "using china's nuclear posture as the opening peg" | When a peg is specified, lead with it. Don't bury it. |
| Long-sentence prose version | "too unbulleted" | Bullets must look like bullets — short, fact-per-line. |
| Short fragment bullets | "avoid smaller sentences. no story arc?" | Earlier session — sentences too compressed lost the arc. |
| Long bullets again | "bullets have to be very short" | Latest calibration: keep bullets compact. Prose for context, bullets for data. |
| Full-length draft | "60% of this length pls" / "keep it 50% of last one's length" | Take length cuts literally. |

## Workflow

1. **Fetch the article.** Read it before drafting.
2. **Identify the peg the user has specified** (or pick the sharpest one). Lead with it.
3. **Build the arc** — setup, development, consequence.
4. **Draft 2–4 sections** of short, data-led bullets.
5. **Write an aphoristic closer** (rule of three works).
6. **Handoff** with publication, author (if warranted), framed value, link.
7. **On revision**, take instructions literally — length, peg, register.

## Template

```
[HOOK: 2-4 sentences. Specific, informal, fact-led. Establishes stakes in the first line.]

[BRIDGE (optional): One sentence pivoting hook to thesis.]

[SECTION 1 LABEL]:

- [Short, fact-led bullet]
- [Comparative or scale bullet]
- [Named-entity bullet]

[SECTION 2 LABEL]:

- [Mechanism]
- [Consequence]
- [What's at stake]

[SECTION 3 LABEL — optional]:

- [What works elsewhere / what's unfinished / what follows]

[APHORISTIC CLOSER: 2-3 short sentences. Rule of three.]

This @swarajyamag [piece / deep-dive / report] [by AUTHOR] is the [sharpest / clearest / best] [diagnosis / explainer / read] you'll [find / read] on [WHAT]: [LINK]
```

## Final Checklist

- [ ] Opener is specific, informal, and hooks.
- [ ] No "wasn't just X" or borrowed-decorative framings.
- [ ] The post has an arc, not a stacked list.
- [ ] Bullets are short and data-led.
- [ ] At least one section uses comparative scale.
- [ ] Length matches what the user asked for.
- [ ] If a peg was specified, the opener leads with it.
- [ ] Aphoristic closer earns its place.
- [ ] Handoff names publication, author, and frames the article.
- [ ] Reads informal-but-informed. Not press release. Not LinkedIn.
